# Copywriting Playbook (Sabri Suby method)
*Consolidated June 13, 2026 from Sabri Suby's copywriting masterclass video. Self contained. A fresh session can read this cold and apply it. Sabri runs the agency King Kong, author of "Sell Like Crazy." Pairs with the offer/business playbook (his other video) and with any customer voice research already on file.*

## How to use this (for the operating session)
The order of operations is the whole game:
1. RESEARCH FIRST. Find out exactly what to say before worrying about how to say it. The game is won here. (See "Forum foraging / Halo strategy".)
2. Find the ONE angle that resonates (test multiple angles, not one angle worded different ways).
3. ASSEMBLE the copy from real customer words using the frameworks below (headline, bullets, the 17 steps).
4. Write at a 6th grade reading level. Then edit ruthlessly so every word earns its place.
5. Adapt the intensity to the brand (see the last section before applying this to a gentle or constrained brand).

Do not skip step 1. Most people do, and that is why most copy fails.

## Part 1. What copywriting actually is
- Copywriting is simply writing words. Two kinds: BRANDED (jingles, cute, makes people giggle) and DIRECT RESPONSE (words that ask for an action: become a lead, or pull out a credit card and buy).
- Direct response is the money skill. The goal: move someone emotionally to do the thing you are asking.
- Everything is copy. The words you speak, the videos you make, hiring, motivating a team, a memo, a sales page. It all starts with the message.
- It is the most valuable money making skill in business. Master it and you never worry about money again.
- Copy is salesmanship multiplied. Selling to one person, done to many at once. Master one to one selling first, then amplify it. An ad is a salesperson calling on 600,000 people at once instead of one.
- Think of your copy as an army of little salespeople you deploy to capture money and bring it home. Their job is to sell, not to be clever or win awards.
- The principles never change because human psychology never changes. Platforms and algorithms change. The skill has a long half life: learn it once, use it on every platform forever. When a new platform appears, you just learn its mechanics and apply the same psychology.

## Part 2. Why this matters more now (the AI era)
- You must be able to tell good copy from bad. AI output looks decent to an untrained eye but is generic. It does maybe 60% of the grunt work; it still needs a qualified human over it (same as a lawyer reviewing a contract AI wrote).
- Better copy is a direct discount on your cost to acquire a customer and your cost per lead, and that saving goes straight to the bottom line.
- Every ad platform is more crowded than ever, and most people are pumping out AI mush. When everyone has the same algorithm finding the same buyers, your only edge is the words, the angle, and how you choose to attack the niche.
- The cost to advertise only goes up. Better, more compelling copy is the only real way to fight that. Creative and copy are the ultimate lever: get 10% more people to click and you raise your profit margin by 10%, with everything else unchanged. No other part of the business gives that much leverage.
- Most people obsess over the wrong things (the new CRM, the new chatbot, shiny objects). The main thing is always the message. Spend your time on what does not change.

## Part 3. The core rules of the craft

### The prospect only cares about themselves
- Do not write about your company, your team, your years in business, your features. Nobody cares. Your product is actually a barrier between the prospect and what they want. They want the OUTCOME, not the product.
- Get into the prospect's shoes. What keeps them up at 3am in a cold sweat. What they do not even tell their partner. Their pains, fears, hopes, dreams.
- The 80/20 test: after writing, check that 80% talks about the prospect (their pain, the big benefit they want) and only 10 to 20% talks about you or how you deliver it.
- Go straight to the problem. (Sabri's breakthrough at 17 selling ink cartridges: he cut the intro about who he was and what company he was from, and opened straight on the problem he solved. He went from worst salesperson to best in a week.) The moment you stop talking about their problem, they tune out.

### Benefits, not features
- Features tell, benefits sell. Sell the end destination, not the work.
- Example: nobody wants a "12 week program with macro tracking and 6am workouts" (that is selling work). They want to drop dress sizes, get the abs, feel the best they ever have. If you sell the work, they will not even want it.
- For every feature, ask: what is the benefit, and the benefit of that benefit. Talk only about how their life gets better.

### Under the fingernail copy
- Describe the prospect's pain in such vivid, specific detail that they cannot deny it. So specific it gets "under their fingernails."
- Then name the one big benefit they would crawl across crushed glass to get.
- When your words are this specific and in their exact language, they think "this person is one of us, they understand my pain better than I do, so they must have the solution." That is when words strike like lightning.

### What to say beats how to say it
- People obsess over how to phrase things (buzzwords, polish). What you say is infinitely more important than how you say it.
- Every market has a bullseye. Hit it dead center and prospects buy emotionally, irrationally, in huge quantities.
- You cannot out write bad research. A weak message written beautifully loses to a strong message written plainly. Plenty of businesses doing tens of millions have rudimentary funnels that real copywriters would laugh at, because they know what to say. Meanwhile "funnel hackers" polishing a turd do 10k a month because they obsess over the wrong thing.

### Write at a 6th grade reading level
- The best authors, biggest YouTubers, and most powerful leaders all write at a 6th grade level or below.
- You never alienate anyone by being too clear. You alienate them by being confusing. Write to the lowest common denominator of your market. Do not let them notice your writing; let them be moved by your message.
- Consumption precedes conversion. The only way to get someone to convert is to get them to consume the message first. Easier reading means more consumption means more sales.
- Simple words. Short sentences. Often 4 to 5 words, then a full stop and a line break. Vary the rhythm (two short sentences then a longer one gives it cadence). A wall of text triggers a gut reaction of "this looks like work."
- A good writer is a good thinker. A lot of the writing happens in your head, on long walks, obsessing over the prospect, until the message is clear. Then you sit and type it.
- Clarity beats cleverness every time.
- TOOL: the Hemingway app (free in a browser, about 19 dollars for desktop). Paste your copy in. It shows the reading level (Flesch Kincaid score) and highlights hard sentences in red and yellow. Fix everything it flags until nothing is flagged. Taking a page from a 9 to 12 grade level down to 6 alone can lift conversions a lot.

### The biggest mistake: talking about yourself
- Most websites say "we have been in business 17 years, our team is huge, we are the trusted experts." It is a declaration about them, not about the prospect. Generic and forgettable. Roughly 1% of businesses have copy that resembles good copy, so it is very easy to stand out.
- Tell: they say "we" and "I" far more than "you."
- The "coliseum keyword": the single biggest search term in a market. When a prospect searches it, everyone is shouting the same "trusted experts, 30 years, friendly team." The prospect cannot tell anyone apart. That sameness is your opportunity.
- THE FIX: record yourself or your best salesperson giving a red hot pitch as if sitting across from a prospect. Handle every question, concern, pain, fear, hope, and dream, then transition into the pitch. It usually runs 30 to 40 minutes. That recording IS what your sales message must do, one to many. Then look at your ads and landing pages: if they say something different from that red hot pitch, burn them. If they somehow still pull leads, they are doing it at 10% of what they could.

## Part 4. Forum foraging and the Halo strategy (the research engine)
This is how you find what to say. It is the most important activity in copywriting.

- Be a fly on the wall where your prospects talk: Facebook groups, forums, Reddit and subreddits, Google reviews (read the 5 star, 3 star, AND 1 star), YouTube comments, competitor reviews.
- Copy every pain, fear, hope, and dream you find into one document. That document is the "Halo strategy." When it is time to write, you are not facing a blank page, you are ASSEMBLING copy from real words.
- Use their exact vernacular. The goal is to sound like one of them, not like a salesperson. People do not like to be sold, but they love to buy.
- TOOL: the "Discussions" Chrome extension adds a forums only tab to Google search, so you get only forum results.
- Do not spend 5 minutes and quit saying "there is nothing for my niche." Spend days. Find the keywords that surface the most forums. Join the groups. Read the competitor reviews.
- This is not beneath you, and it is not once and done. Redo it every month, quarter, and year. Never go more than 6 months. Founders get jaded, get pulled off the front lines, and lose touch with what the market actually wants. Markets shift constantly now (with AI, almost daily).
- "Enter the conversation already taking place in the prospect's mind." (Robert Collier, copywriter from ~100 years ago.) Do not try to CREATE desire. CHANNEL the desire that already exists toward your product.
- How you know you are saying the right thing: test multiple different ANGLES (not the same message reworded). One will be an outlier that resonates far more than the rest. That is your "what to say." Only then obsess over how to say it.

## Part 5. Funnels: every step has one job
- Not every step sells. On most platforms a person cannot buy directly from an ad (they see a Google or Facebook ad, they cannot check out there). So the ad's job is not to sell the product. The ad's job is to SELL THE CLICK.
- The landing page's job is then to generate the lead or make the sale.
- Look at every step of the funnel and ask: what is the single job of this step. Write only for that job.

### How to sell the click
- A big specific benefit that calls out the audience, plus intrigue.
- The more advertised to your market is, the more specific the claim must be. Not "here is how to lose weight" (seen 70,000 times) but "the weird berry from the Himalayas that turns your body into a fat burning furnace" or "the 2 minute exercise 45 minutes before bed that builds muscle automatically."
- Mental model: the market is a raging river of prospects. Your job is to channel some of that river to your product by hijacking attention. The prospect is a sleepwalking zombie, a lethargic person on the couch covered in Doritos. It is freezing outside and you have to get them up and running around the block. How big and specific a benefit, and how much curiosity, would that take? You are competing with the entire phone full of unlimited entertainment.

### What drives a click
- Curiosity (the biggest), but not blind curiosity. Make it specific to your market. "What supermodels do before bed" is blind (brings everyone). "The skin routine supermodels do 45 minutes before bed" brings the right people and pairs curiosity with the benefit.
- Shock / pattern interrupt.
- Implied benefit and direct benefit.
- Vanity, fear, self interest (richer, faster, better looking, more attractive). It must better their situation. Then layer curiosity on top so they click to find out.

## Part 6. Overcoming skepticism
- Skepticism is at an all time high. Low barriers to entry mean many unqualified people sell things badly, buyers get burned, and everyone gets jaded. So a legitimate business has a hard job standing out.
- Name the alternatives the prospect has already tried, and explain why each one failed THEM. (Weight loss example: "you have probably tried paleo, intermittent fasting, keto, you lose a few pounds then put more back on, because none of them fix the root problem, your hormones / thyroid.")
- Sell the way the market wants to buy, not the way it "should" buy. ("Eat at a deficit and move more" is true but unsellable to a jaded market.)
- The antidote to skepticism is VALUE. Make the ad and funnel valuable on their own. Leave the prospect better off than you found them whether or not they buy. Give away a real piece of the result inside the ad. Then they think "if the free stuff is this good, imagine the paid." Make your free better than your competitors' paid and you take over the market. No headline trick beats real value in the copy.
- Attack skepticism head on. Sell against the alternatives, do not pretend they do not exist. "A buyer is a buyer is a buyer": the people buying from you also buy from everyone else, so know what else they buy and sell against it.
- After writing, it should read so well that only a lunatic would not buy. If it is that good and they still do not buy, it is because they do not believe you. Handle the objections directly in the copy, before they arise. You already know the objections from the red hot pitch recording. Show the exact situation ("this person tried X, it did not work, then with our thing they got Y"), so the reader nods along and every objection is handled before they reach the price.

## Part 7. Headlines
- Story: Norah Hayden's book "Astrological Love" flopped (sold ~2,000, dumped at dollar stores). A publisher reprinted it with zero changes to the inside, just a new title and cover: "How to Satisfy a Woman Every Time and Have Her Beg for More." It sold 2.3 million copies in 18 months and hit the New York Times bestseller list. Same content, different packaging.
- About 5 times more people read the headline than the body copy. David Ogilvy: once you have written the headline, you have spent 80 cents of your dollar. So spend roughly 80% of your time on the headline.
- Study where the best headline writers are by following money and attention. It used to be supermarket gossip magazines (National Enquirer). Now it is the most visited sites: TMZ, the New York Times, Lad Bible. They are masters at hijacking attention. We are all "monkeys in disguise," reptilian brains. Learn the psychology there, then wrap it in your industry and product.
- What those sites do: always use numbers; words like exposed, shocked, leak, wild details emerge; an ugly iPhone looking mid action photo; 3 to 4 gripping words; they elicit an emotion in the first 3 seconds. Swipe and deploy into your market.
- "My prospect is different / too educated" is wrong. Sabri runs blind split tests across thousands of niches; primal psychology beats the client's own "sophisticated" ads every time. They are the same chimp in disguise: they want to be richer, fitter, more attractive, with results fast.

### The 5 headline essentials
1. GRAB them. The headline does not tell the whole story, it grabs them by the throat and demands attention. A 3 to 4 word "grabber" (most New York Times headlines are 3 to 4 words). The sub headline expands.
2. USE NUMBERS. They give the mind something tangible (this is why listicles work). They open a loop the prospect must close. They work even in sophisticated markets.
3. BURNING CURIOSITY. Buzzwords, shocking or alarming framing, a twist or qualifier ("7 things no property investor would dare tell you"), or address the biggest objection ("how to start an online business even if you are a complete beginner with no idea where to start").
4. WHAT IS IN IT FOR THEM. A big specific benefit. Catch more flies with honey. Do not be negative or fancy. Promise the big benefit.
5. BE SPECIFIC. Not "lose weight" but "lose 12 to 15 kgs in the next 9 weeks without eating like a rabbit or doing endless cardio." The more jaded the market, the deeper the specificity.

### Headline formulas (swipe and deploy)
1. [Number] ways to achieve [desirable thing] without [undesirable thing]. Example: "6 ways to get washboard abs without doing a single situp."
2. Do [difficult thing] in [specific time] even if [shortcoming]. Example: "Pay off your mortgage in 7 years even if you have a modest income."
3. Achieve [desired thing] like [an expert] even without [expected requirement]. Example: "Paint a masterpiece like Picasso without ever having a single lesson."
4. How to eliminate [biggest problem] without [thing they hate] within [time frame]. Example: "How to eliminate your muffin top without giving up the foods you love within 21 days."

## Part 8. Bullet copy (fascinations)
- Little teaser nuggets that sell either the product itself or the act of consuming the rest of the message. Each one should be able to justify the purchase on its own. Hyper specific to an exact pain point.
- They are one of the most read parts of any sales message because they are scannable. Typical reading path: headline, sub headline, scroll to the price/CTA, scroll back up, read the sub headlines, then spend the most time on the bullets.
- Crank curiosity to 10 out of 10. Tease and tantalize, an itch they have to scratch. Do not barrage them with claims, play with them. Any single great bullet could be tested as a headline.
- Claude Hopkins: "Curiosity is among the strongest human incentives."

### Fascination formulas (swipe and deploy)
1. "You need [X]? Wrong." (State a common belief, then argue against it.) Example: "You need to eat at a calorie deficit to lose weight. Wrong."
2. "[Number] ways to [Y]." Example: "5 ways to meet single women in Melbourne."
3. "Where to find [Y]." Example: "Where to find the most ravenous, hyperactive buyers online."
4. "How to eliminate [X]." Example: "How to stop joint pain forever."
5. "What you should never do." (Taps fear of loss; people are more motivated to avoid losing than to gain.)
6. "Say goodbye to [frustration]." Example: "Say goodbye to calorie counting and hour long cardio sessions."
7. "The truth about [Y]." Example: "The dirty truth about fish oil revealed." (Timeless, still works.)

## Part 9. Long form vs short form
- Long form beats short form, with one caveat: never long for the sake of long.
- The people who will not read long copy will not read short copy either, because they are not buyers. BUYERS read every word: they want every objection handled and every question answered. Write for the buyer.
- Your copy has to replicate that 40 minute red hot pitch. You cannot do that in three paragraphs. More expensive offer means a longer pitch.
- Proof: Sabri's book funnel page is roughly 8,500 to 12,000 words. The longer he made it, the higher the conversion rate, average order value, and return on ad spend.
- But the first thing you do after writing is EDIT and cut. Get it all out, then make every word fight for its place. As long as it needs to be, never rambling. Keep it entertaining, "read or die," wink at the prospect so it is fun to read, not a chore.
- When someone says "who will read all this," the answer is the buyers. Do not ask casuals, friends, or the receptionist for feedback (they are not in the market). Look at your contact forms, emails, and sales calls; you will find your message is not nearly long enough to address everything that actually comes up.

## Part 10. The 17 step sales message (master template)
Use for ads, landing pages, video sales letters, blog posts, any message that asks for action.
1. CALL OUT YOUR AUDIENCE. Use eyebrow copy (a small line above the headline). Example: "Attention property investors who want to become millionaires." Answers the first question: is this for me.
2. DEMAND ATTENTION. A big, bold, specific benefit in the headline, something they would crawl across crushed glass for. Do not be cute.
3. BACK UP THE PROMISE IN THE SUB HEADLINE. A straightforward expansion that pulls them into the body. Never tell the whole story; the whole page is built to sell the next line.
4. CREATE IRRESISTIBLE INTRIGUE. The leading copy panel below the sub headline. Flame desire and curiosity, and crank curiosity again in the bullets.
5. SHINE A FLOODLIGHT ON THE PAIN. Describe their pain so vividly they assume you have the solution. Kick the bruised knee, pour vinegar in the wound, agitate and amplify the pain. People move away from pain harder than they move toward pleasure.
6. PROVIDE A UNIQUE SOLUTION. Prove it is different from everything they have tried. Dismantle each prior solution and show why it failed them.
7. SHOW YOUR CREDENTIALS. Undeniable proof: case studies, testimonials, video testimonials, stats and data.
8. DETAIL THE BENEFITS. List every feature, and for each feature write two matching benefits. Then talk only about the benefits and how life gets better.
9. SOCIAL PROOF. How many bought, reviews, outcomes, average results, video testimonials, screenshots, invoices, bank statements. Show, do not tell.
10. MAKE YOUR IRRESISTIBLE OFFER (the most important step). Crystal clear, value packed, clearly stating how it delivers the end outcome they want most.
11. ADD SWEETENERS. Bonuses. Each bonus should feel worth the whole price on its own. Sweet spot is 3 to 4 bonuses.
12. STACK THE VALUE. Add up the total value of the offer plus all bonuses to build the highest believable price.
13. REVEAL YOUR PRICE. Position it as a small fraction of that stacked total value.
14. INJECT SCARCITY. Real scarcity only (true capacity or stock limits). Give a reason to act now; delay is the death of the sale. Example: if you could realistically only serve 15 of 100 new clients, say you have capacity for 15 and this message is going to thousands.
15. GIVE A POWERFUL GUARANTEE. Risk is always in the transaction; either you carry it or the prospect does. Carry it. Not having a guarantee inflates your cost to acquire a customer far more than refunds ever would. Removing the risk can multiply buyers.
16. CALL TO ACTION. Tell, do not ask. An embedded command. Walk them through exactly what to do step by step ("click the button below, fill in your details, you will be taken to...").
17. CLOSE WITH A PS. One of the most read parts of any sales message. Lead with a negative (keep suffering with the problem...) and end with a positive (or click below and get the outcome). You can add a PPS. People quote the PS back to you on sales calls.

## Part 11. The future of copywriting (working with AI)
- AI gives 1000x more output. Copywriters are not going away; the old manual way is.
- The new role is "copy chief": you direct AI, assemble and judge the copy (this is good, this is wrong, this is not different enough, this is not compliant). Spend your own time on the BIG IDEA, the counterintuitive angle, the uncontested white space in the market.
- Do not ask AI for the big idea. Find it yourself by studying what everyone else is doing, then go where they are not. Use AI as a cub and a soundboard for the grunt work, with a human at the helm.
- Everyone prompts AI the same way ("you are a world class copywriter, write a high converting sales page"), so everyone's AI copy looks the same. Zig while they zag.
- Best workflow: find the winner first (the winning headline and copy from real testing), THEN feed that winner to AI for variations ("give me 5 variations of this for this avatar"). Give it the best DNA; never let it run free from scratch. Raw AI copy is generic, not "under the fingernail," and converts worse unless a skilled human shapes it.
- People who understand human psychology become MORE valuable in the AI era, because each one now produces far more (one person writing 100 ads a day, a sales letter a day).

## Quick checklist (run any piece of copy through this)
- [ ] Did I research first and pull real customer words (Halo strategy)?
- [ ] Is it 80% about the prospect, 20% or less about us?
- [ ] Benefits and outcomes, not features and work?
- [ ] Does it name and amplify a specific pain in their words (under the fingernail)?
- [ ] Does it handle the objections and beat the alternatives they have tried?
- [ ] Headline: grabs in 3 to 4 words, specific, curious, with a number where possible?
- [ ] Does each bullet tease enough to stand alone?
- [ ] Is there real value given for free, so it beats skepticism?
- [ ] 6th grade reading level (checked in Hemingway)? Short sentences, simple words?
- [ ] Clear single call to action, told not asked?
- [ ] For a full sales message: are all 17 steps present?
- [ ] Edited ruthlessly so every word earns its place?

## Adaptation layer: applying this to Cal's brands
This is the bridge from Sabri's system to the actual clients. The principles all transfer; the intensity and the heavy machinery do not always.

- This system was built for PAID direct response at scale (long sales pages, video sales letters, hard scarcity, aggressive guarantees, big ad budgets). Cal's current work is mostly ORGANIC social content for warm, small, brand constrained clients. So:
  - The PRINCIPLES port straight to captions, hooks, and reels today: their words not ours, pain and outcome first, benefits not features, specificity, curiosity, one job per asset, 6th grade clarity, value that beats skepticism.
  - The HEAVY MACHINERY (12,000 word sales pages, countdown scarcity, value stacks) is for when a client has a real funnel and an ad budget. That is the Zentara paid future more than this week's post.
- Match the intensity to the brand. "Grab them by the throat, pour vinegar in the wound" suits a weight loss funnel. For a gentle wellness brand or a warm local salon, name the pain with empathy, do not agitate it aggressively.
- Honor each client's locked rules. Example: Unik captions are locked by the owner (Thuy) to short, playful, and strictly factual, so wild hype claims and big bold promises do not go on Unik. The "their words, pain first" principle still works inside that box.
- The customer voice research already done for Unik, Nail'd It Spa, and Salvia Lion is the "what to say" half. This playbook is the "how to say it" half. Use them together: pull the real quotes from each client's customer-voice-research file, then assemble with the frameworks here.
- Source rule: every stat, claim, and number in finished client copy must be true and backed. Sabri's specificity tactics are about being vivid, never about inventing claims.
